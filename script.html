Opening

  • I want to walk you through a project I did at JPMorganChase called NPA, short for New Product Assessment.
  • It's a firmwide platform that consolidates two legacy processes — NBIA and PSCM — into one system for assessing risk and approving new products or product changes.
  • Four groups use it day to day: the people initiating something new, the reviewers, the risk SMEs who evaluate specific risk dimensions, and governance leads who need a clean audit trail for exams.
  • This matters because at a bank that size, a slow or inconsistent approval process doesn't just frustrate people internally — it shows up as slower time to market and as governance gaps that regulators care about.

The Problem

  • Every line of business had built its own version of this process over time, so there was no shared data layer and no consistent workflow.
  • An initiator could answer the same five risk questions in three different systems, and a reviewer would see that same information formatted three different ways.
  • Nobody — not initiators, not reviewers, not governance — had a single view of what was in flight, where it was stuck, or what risk it carried.
  • And underneath all of that, different lines of business were using the same words to mean different things, which is a quiet but serious problem in a regulated environment.

My Responsibility

  • I was the senior UX designer on this, and I owned it end to end — discovery, information architecture, wireframes, high-fidelity design, usability testing — and I stayed on through the build to review what actually shipped against the spec.
  • I worked directly with product and with risk and compliance stakeholders across multiple lines of business, and I embedded risk and compliance partners into design critiques so we weren't validating decisions after the fact.
  • Nobody else took over a phase of this from me — that continuity mattered, because it meant nothing got lost in a hand-off.

Discovery

  • I needed to understand how four very different roles experienced the same process, so I ran about twenty moderated interviews with initiators, reviewers, risk SMEs, and governance leads across lines of business.
  • I mapped current-state and future-state journeys for both NBIA and PSCM to see every handoff and every point where something got duplicated or delayed.
  • I also did a heuristic audit of the legacy tools, which is where a lot of the duplicated fields and conflicting terminology became visible on paper, not just anecdotally.
  • And I ran workshops with risk and compliance to get everyone aligned on one firmwide risk taxonomy before I touched a single screen.
  • What came out of that was four things people kept saying, almost verbatim: "I answer the same five questions in three systems." "I don't know where my submission is or who's blocking it." "Risk reviewers and product owners speak different languages." And, "governance needs an audit trail, not a static report."

The Turning Point

  • The insight that reframed the whole project was realizing consolidation is a UX problem before it's a technology problem.
  • Replacing the systems was the easy part on paper — the hard part was getting four lines of business to agree on one vocabulary, one risk taxonomy, and one shared idea of what "approved" actually means.
  • Once I saw that, I stopped thinking about this as "redesign four tools into one" and started thinking about it as "design one shared model that can flex by role."

Design Decisions

  • The first principle I worked from was ask once, reuse everywhere — if the firm already has an answer to a question somewhere, the initiator shouldn't have to type it again. So the intake questionnaire auto-scopes to NBIA or PSCM based on early answers and pre-fills from data the firm already holds. Show: New Risk Intake Form
  • Second, I made status visible by default instead of something you had to go dig for. Every role — initiator, reviewer, governance — gets a tailored view that answers "what's in flight, where is it, who's it waiting on," without anyone having to ask. Show: Home Dashboard
  • Third, and this was the trickiest one, I standardized risk without flattening it. There's one taxonomy underneath, enforced at the system level, but the presentation adapts — initiators see plain-language prompts, risk SMEs see the underlying rubric and the evidence trail behind it. Show: Risk Taxonomy Board
  • I also designed an examiner readiness report that pulls straight from the same underlying data as the risk workspace, so nothing gets re-keyed for reporting — which matters a lot when you're trying to close a reconciliation gap. Show: Examiner Readiness Report

Collaboration & Validation

  • I ran three rounds of moderated usability testing on mid-fidelity prototypes with actual initiators and reviewers before engineering committed to the build.
  • Risk and compliance weren't just reviewing at the end — they were in the design critiques with me, so when something didn't hold up under a governance lens, I heard about it early enough to change it cheaply.
  • I used AI tools deliberately, but with limits. Claude helped me get a first pass at themes across twenty-plus interview transcripts, and Figma AI helped me explore layout variations faster on the highest-branching screens. But anything touching accessibility decisions or the risk-taxonomy language, I kept that off AI entirely, because "the tool suggested it" doesn't hold up under compliance scrutiny.

Outcomes & Reflection

  • After launch, we saw roughly a 40% reduction in overall cycle time and about a 55% drop in redundant data entry, based on usage data on the new submission workflow.
  • Status-check support requests dropped noticeably too, which tracked with what we heard in research — "where's my submission" was an emotionally loud question, even if it wasn't the flashiest problem to solve.
  • If I look back at what actually made this work, it's that staying on the project end to end kept the research alive all the way to what shipped — nothing got diluted in a hand-off.

Opening

  • VentureBacked is a community platform built to connect founders raising early-stage capital with angel investors and emerging fund GPs looking to source deals.
  • It's a two-sided marketplace, which means the product only works if both sides trust it enough to actually use it.
  • Founders were already spraying decks into inboxes they hoped were real, and investors were already drowning in deal flow with no reliable way to sort signal from noise. The existing options — LinkedIn, AngelList, cold intros on Twitter — were adding noise, not solving that.

The Problem

  • There were really four things working against the product before I got involved: matching that nobody trusted, profile creation that caused people to drop off mid-signup on both sides, a visual identity that didn't read as credible in a category where credibility is the whole product, and no design system, which was slowing engineering down on every new feature.
  • Trust and speed were fighting each other — the more information you asked people for up front to build trust, the more of them you lost before they ever saw value.

My Responsibility

  • I was the lead UX and product designer for about six months, and I owned this end to end — discovery research, interaction and visual design, and I built the design system from scratch, foundations through documentation.
  • I ran the research program myself, and I worked with engineering on a weekly cadence so the design system wasn't just a deliverable I handed off — it was something we built together as we went.

Discovery

  • I needed to understand what "trustworthy" actually meant to two very different audiences, so I ran eighteen moderated interviews with founders, angel investors, and emerging fund GPs.
  • I mapped separate journeys for founders raising and investors sourcing, because those are genuinely different mental models even though they're using the same platform.
  • I did a competitive teardown of nine adjacent products — LinkedIn, AngelList, Signal, Crunchbase — to see where the category's existing conventions were actually working against users.
  • Then I validated what I was hearing qualitatively with a broader survey, and used Dovetail to tag everything so the whole team could search the research, not just me.
  • Four things came out clearly: "I don't need more intros, I need the right intros." "Tell me in ten seconds if this person is worth my time." "If I can't sign up in three minutes, I'm out." And, "it has to look like it belongs in this category" — meaning visual polish wasn't decoration, it was doing real credibility work.

The Turning Point

  • The line that reframed the project for me was "I don't need more intros, I need the right intros" — because it told me the bottleneck wasn't the volume of connections, it was confidence in the ones people already had.
  • That shifted my whole design direction away from "help people find more matches" and toward "help people evaluate a match in seconds, and build trust into the interface itself instead of bolting it on later."

Design Decisions

  • I rebuilt profiles around a glanceable header — stage, thesis, check size, traction, last activity — all above the fold, so someone could size up a match in seconds instead of reading a full bio. Show: Founder Profile
  • I cut onboarding down to three lightweight steps and pushed the heavier asks — full deck, detailed thesis, traction numbers — to later moments where they'd actually help the user, instead of front-loading everything and losing people before they saw value. Show: Choose Your Role
  • The decision I'd call out as the trickiest one: I treated trust as a UI primitive, not a feature. Verification badges, source-of-data labels, "last verified" timestamps — those show up as first-class components everywhere someone evaluates another member, not as an add-on screen. Show: Discovery Tags
  • Because engineering velocity was one of the original problems, I built the design system in layers — foundations as Figma variables mirrored in code, a full component library with every state covered, patterns for the recurring compositions, and documentation with the reasoning behind each decision, not just the "how."

Collaboration & Validation

  • I ran weekly design critiques with engineering before anything went to build, specifically so the design system held up in practice, not just in Figma.
  • I brought concepts back to founders and investors from the research pool for review, and ran three rounds of usability testing plus unmoderated Maze studies to catch comprehension issues before investing in high fidelity.
  • When there was pressure to add more fields or features, I used the three principles from research — signal over noise, trust by default, respect people's time — as the filter for what got in and what didn't.

Outcomes & Reflection

  • What we ended up with was a product that reads as one coherent system end to end, a genuinely lighter onboarding flow, and a design system that let engineering ship new features against a documented library instead of starting from scratch every time.
  • The thing I'd point to as the real win is that the research protected the product from becoming a clone of what everyone else in the category was already doing — "signal over volume" wasn't something a competitive scan alone would have surfaced.

  • I worked on a Salesforce Sales Cloud implementation for CrowdStrike, the cybersecurity company — I want to be precise about that, because it's easy to hear "CrowdStrike" and assume I was designing their security product. I wasn't. I was rebuilding how their own sales team used Salesforce.
  • CrowdStrike was pushing to scale sales to five billion in annual recurring revenue, but their Salesforce experience hadn't evolved to match that ambition.
  • The people using it every day were sales reps across enterprise, mid-market, and SMB deals, sales managers, and SDRs.

The Problem

  • Reps were toggling across six or more tabs to get through a single customer interaction, and that context-switching was costing an estimated two to three hours a day per rep.
  • A lot of critical deal information was living in spreadsheets and email instead of Salesforce, which meant pipeline data wasn't reliable and forecasting suffered because of it.
  • The platform wasn't using the AI capabilities that were already available to surface next-best actions or flag at-risk deals — so reps got summaries, not guidance.
  • On top of that, a chunk of the screens failed WCAG 2.1 color contrast requirements and had inconsistent keyboard navigation, so it wasn't accessible to everyone using it.

My Responsibility

  • I was the lead UX consultant on this, embedded with the Salesforce design team.
  • I led the research myself — the interviews, the shadowing, the heuristic audit — and then facilitated design sprints and built the high-fidelity Figma prototypes that went through three rounds of testing before handoff.

Discovery

  • I ran eighteen moderated interviews with reps, managers, and SDRs across different deal stages, and shadowed eight reps directly during live customer calls and pipeline reviews, because I wanted to see the tab-switching problem happen, not just hear about it.
  • I built future-state journey maps for five distinct personas, and ran a heuristic audit against Nielsen's ten heuristics across more than forty screens — that alone surfaced eighty-seven distinct pain points.
  • I also pulled Salesforce usage telemetry to see where people were actually dropping off and what features were getting used versus ignored.
  • Three things people said stuck with me: "I have the data, I just can't find it fast enough." "I trust my spreadsheet more than Salesforce." And, "I'd love AI that helps me prioritize, not just summarize."

The Turning Point

  • The turning point was realizing the spreadsheet behavior wasn't a training problem or a discipline problem — it was a rational response to a system reps didn't trust.
  • That reframed the whole project. It stopped being "add more capabilities to Salesforce" and became "make Salesforce faster and more trustworthy than the workaround people had already built for themselves."

Design Decisions

  • The first move was consolidating that six-tab workflow into a single deal workspace with contextual panels, so reps could see everything relevant to a deal in one place instead of hunting across tabs.
  • Second, I surfaced AI-driven next-best-action recommendations — follow-up timing, deal health scores, at-risk flags — directly inside that same deal view, because research told me reps wanted something that told them what to do next, not something that summarized what they already knew.
  • Third, I rebuilt the component library to be accessible from the ground up — proper color contrast, full keyboard navigation, ARIA labeling, and focus management throughout. That wasn't a separate accessibility pass at the end, it was baked into the same component work everything else was built on.

Collaboration & Validation

  • I ran baseline usability testing on the current-state experience first, so I had real performance numbers to compare against, not just impressions.
  • Then I went through three rounds of moderated testing on the high-fidelity prototypes, iterating with the Salesforce design team between each round before we handed anything to engineering.
  • The AI integration only earned its place in the design when it removed a step reps were already doing manually — the ones that added a new interaction for reps to learn didn't make the cut.

Outcomes & Reflection

  • In the first sixty days after launch, platform adoption among reps was up 34%, average time on core sales tasks — pipeline updates, deal reviews, follow-up logging — was down 41%, and CRM data quality scores were up 28% because reps were entering information directly instead of routing around the system.
  • Looking back, the real lesson was that reps didn't need more capability, they needed faster paths to the capability they already had — that's a different design problem than the one it looks like on the surface.

Opening

  • I worked with Autodesk to redesign the platform their customer support agents use — an internal tool, not something end customers ever see, but it directly shapes the support experience customers get.
  • The agents were spread across the US, India, Ireland, and the Philippines, working technical support, licensing, billing, and enterprise account management.
  • Autodesk had accumulated a collection of aging, siloed systems through years of acquisitions and incremental additions, and agents were toggling between seven or more applications per customer interaction, manually copying information between them.

The Problem

  • Beyond the raw number of tools, there was no unified search across support documentation, so knowledge access itself was broken.
  • Agents had no visibility into a customer's product history or account health at the start of an interaction — they were starting cold every time.
  • And there was no real feedback loop for performance beyond a CSAT score at the end — nothing in-call that told an agent, in the moment, how they were doing or what a strong agent would do differently.

My Responsibility

  • I was the lead UX consultant on this, and I led the research program design and execution, facilitated synthesis workshops with the design and product teams, and ran weekly co-creation workshops directly with agents.
  • I worked closely with Autodesk product managers and engineers throughout, and I made a point of including agents in the ideation itself, not just bringing them in at the end to validate something I'd already built.

Discovery

  • I ran shadow sessions in person across all four locations, watching real customer interactions as they happened, because a support conversation in the India center looked genuinely different from one in the US.
  • I ran twenty-four contextual interviews with agents across tiers, tenure, and specialization, and built a full service blueprint of the agent journey from case intake to resolution across phone, chat, and email.
  • In synthesis, I clustered over four hundred data points from that research into themes with the team, and layered in six months of operational metrics — handle time, first-contact resolution, CSAT by tier — to ground everything in more than just what people said.
  • What came out of that was something I hadn't expected walking in: veteran agents had built elaborate personal systems — physical notes, bookmarks, copy-paste scripts — to compensate for what the tools didn't give them, and none of that was visible to management or something a new agent could inherit.

The Turning Point

  • The insight that shaped everything downstream was that shadow infrastructure is a signal, not a problem — when people build their own workarounds, they're showing you exactly what the official system is failing to provide.
  • That reframed the opportunity: if the platform could codify what the best agents were already doing into the interface itself — smart defaults, contextual guidance, a unified customer history — it could close a meaningful chunk of the experience gap between a new hire and a veteran, instead of treating that gap as purely a training problem.

Design Decisions

  • The first piece was a single customer view — licenses, case history, account health, recent interactions, account tier — that loaded automatically with zero manual lookup, replacing the cold start every agent used to face.
  • Second, I built unified search across support docs, internal wikis, and escalation playbooks that surfaced content contextually based on the case category and customer profile, instead of making agents hunt across systems.
  • Third, I designed guided resolution flows for the twenty highest-volume issue types, essentially encoding what veteran agents already knew into a repeatable path — the direct answer to the shadow-infrastructure insight.
  • And I added real-time sentiment indicators in the case view — escalation risk, frustration cues — so agents got a feedback signal in the moment instead of only after the call, in a CSAT score.

Collaboration & Validation

  • Co-creation was central here — I ran weekly workshops with product managers, engineers, and agents together, and having agents in the room for ideation, not just testing, produced designs that matched how people actually thought about their work, not how a stakeholder assumed they thought about it.
  • I validated in stages — low-fidelity wireframes with agents first to check direction, then three rounds of interactive Figma prototypes tested with agents across different tiers and locations before anything went to engineering.
  • The multi-location piece wasn't optional for me — the pain points in India looked meaningfully different from the US, and if I'd only tested with one region, I'd have shipped something that didn't actually work globally.

Outcomes & Reflection

  • In the first quarter after rollout, average handle time was down 38%, first-contact resolution was up 22%, and new-agent ramp time to proficiency dropped from about eight weeks to roughly three.
  • The biggest thing I took from this project is that codifying expertise is a design problem as much as a training problem — you can put a veteran's judgment into the interface itself, and that changes outcomes faster than a training program alone would.

Opening

  • I worked on PruXpress, Prudential's advisor portal — the tool thousands of licensed financial advisors use every day to get product information, compliance documents, sales materials, and client management tools.
  • This is a regulated financial services environment, so when advisors can't find the right document quickly, that's not just an annoyance, it's a compliance and revenue risk.
  • The portal had grown organically over years without a coherent information architecture strategy, and by the time I got involved, that had become the core problem underneath almost everything else advisors complained about.

The Problem

  • Locating a specific product resource, compliance document, or training material took excessive time and often just failed outright.
  • Search returned broad, unranked results with no contextual filtering, so advisors were scanning and giving up rather than finding what they needed.
  • The menu structure reflected Prudential's internal organizational hierarchy, not how advisors actually thought about their own work.
  • And outdated content sat right alongside current content with no way to tell them apart, which in a regulated industry is a real trust and compliance problem, not just a UX annoyance.

My Responsibility

  • I was the UX designer and researcher on this engagement, working through Cognizant alongside Prudential stakeholders.
  • I owned the research that diagnosed the IA failures, designed the new task-oriented architecture, built and tested the prototypes, and delivered the high-fidelity design with accessibility and developer specs.

Discovery

  • I mapped the full technology environment and content lifecycle first, then ran twenty-two moderated interviews with advisors about their objectives, tasks, and pain points.
  • The core method here was card sorting — open and closed sorts with forty advisors using Optimal Workshop — to understand how they actually mentally categorized all this content.
  • Once I had a proposed structure, I validated it with tree testing across thirty advisors before any visual design started, and I ran timed task-success studies on the current portal across eight high-frequency tasks to get a real baseline.
  • Analytics backed up what I was hearing — 67% of search sessions ended in zero clicks, meaning people were scanning results and just leaving rather than clicking through.

The Turning Point

  • The card sort data was the turning point, and it did something research doesn't always get to do — it settled an internal disagreement. Every stakeholder had an opinion about how the navigation should be organized, but the sort data represented forty actual advisors' mental models, not internal preference.
  • It showed advisors organize everything by task — what they're trying to accomplish — not by Prudential's product-line structure. That single finding invalidated the existing IA completely and justified starting over rather than patching what was there.

Design Decisions

  • I rebuilt the navigation around task-oriented categories — "prepare for a client meeting," "manage a policy," "get compliant" — instead of product-line categories, so the structure matched how advisors actually think about their day.
  • I redesigned search to be predictive with contextual filtering by content type and date, ranked by relevance, and put "last verified" dates directly on search results — that came straight out of the trust concerns advisors raised in interviews.
  • I added visible publication and "last reviewed" dates across all content, with callouts for recently updated material, which addressed the trust erosion problem directly.
  • I also built a real onboarding path for new advisors — curated checklists, video walkthroughs, quick-reference guides — because research showed advisors under twelve months were relying almost entirely on colleagues or phone support instead of the portal itself.
  • Getting the IA right wasn't one attempt — two proposed structures failed tree testing before I landed on one that worked, and catching that at the tree-testing stage instead of after high-fidelity design was a real efficiency win.

Collaboration & Validation

  • I ran a full-day design thinking workshop with Prudential stakeholders and advisor representatives together, which helped ground the redesign in evidence everyone in the room had seen firsthand.
  • I tested paper prototypes in rapid cycles before moving to digital, then validated functional Figma prototypes covering all the primary advisor journeys in moderated sessions, measuring task success quantitatively at each stage.
  • The content freshness indicators weren't originally in scope — advisors raised the need for them directly in interviews, and adding them turned into one of the highest-rated improvements in post-launch feedback.

  • Task success on content-findability tasks improved 71% against the pre-redesign baseline, average time on task dropped 52% across the eight most frequent advisor journeys, and support call volume dropped 33% within ninety days of launch.

Opening

  • I designed Pulse, an inventory management app for Walmart, used by frontline associates, department leads, and store managers across their store network.
  • The people using this are on the floor during a shift — often wearing gloves, moving between locations, sometimes in loud or dimly lit parts of the store.
  • The problem it replaced was a manual, paper-based inventory adjustment process that was slow, error-prone, and essentially impossible to audit.

The Problem

  • Paper logs got reconciled with the central system hours or sometimes days later, so there was a real lag between what was physically on the shelf and what the system showed.
  • Department managers had no real-time visibility across their sections — no way to check stock levels without physically walking the floor or making a phone call.
  • Tracking spanned multiple locations with multiple log sheets and no single source of truth, and time-tracking was completely disconnected from the actual task someone was doing, so there was no way to analyze labor cost by task type.
  • All of that showed up downstream as out-of-stock errors, customer complaints, and unnecessary replenishment costs.

My Responsibility

  • I was the UX designer and researcher on this, working through Cognizant, and I led the app's design along with an immersive, store-embedded research program.
  • I ran the associate interviews myself, mapped the full workflow, facilitated the stakeholder review workshops, and stayed through the pilot rollout and the design iterations that came out of it.

Discovery

  • I spent three days embedded in two stores during peak and off-peak hours, watching inventory adjustment happen in real time, not just hearing about it afterward.
  • I ran twenty-eight interviews with associates across roles, tenure, and departments, and mapped the end-to-end inventory adjustment process in detail — every step, every handoff, every tool involved.
  • I specifically researched the physical conditions the app would be used under — gloved hands, bright or dim lighting, noisy environments, people moving between locations — because a design that only worked in ideal conditions wasn't going to work here at all.
  • What came out of that: associates needed interactions they could complete in a five-second pause, not a sustained, multi-step task. Experienced associates were genuinely skeptical of digital tools because they'd seen slow, unreliable ones come and go, and had learned paper was more dependable. And discrepancies were often spotted by associates just passing through a section, not only the person who owned it — so this needed to work socially, not just individually.

The Turning Point

  • The turning point was recognizing associates already had a working alternative in paper, which meant adoption wasn't going to happen by mandate — it had to be earned by being faster.

Design Decisions

  • The core interaction was one-tap inventory adjustment — scan a SKU, see the current record, use one large increment or decrement control, confirm, and it syncs to the central system immediately. The whole thing completes in under ten seconds instead of the old hours-long paper-to-system lag.
  • I gave department managers a real-time, multi-location dashboard so they could see stock levels across their section and flag anything below threshold without leaving the stockroom or picking up a phone.
  • I built cross-department discrepancy flagging so any associate passing through could flag an issue, with it routing automatically to the right department lead — that took the informal peer-spotting behavior I'd seen in research and turned it into an actual structured workflow.
  • I tied time-tracking to specific task types — stocking, inventory count, discrepancy resolution — which created the first reliable data set connecting labor hours to actual operational tasks at the store level.
  • Every interactive element had to work with a single large touch target, because gloved hands and single-handed use ruled out small buttons or precision gestures anywhere in the core workflow.

  • I ran iterative wireframe review workshops with store managers, operations leadership, and IT to align on functionality and technical constraints before investing in high-fidelity design.
